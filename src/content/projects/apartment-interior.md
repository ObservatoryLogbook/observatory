## Background
I moved into my new apartment in August 2026. I love my apartment! 

It is located on the 7th floor (European counting of floors), and faces South and West. I have a balcony that wraps around the apartment, and particularly the kitchen and living room face West with large panoramic windows. 


## Original idea

The original idea was for a Japandi-inspired interior: a natural, muted colour palette, simple forms, natural materials and relatively few objects. I wanted the apartment to feel calm and serene, with enough visual space that it could easily be kept orderly and uncluttered.

It was never supposed to become a perfectly curated or anonymous Japandi interior, however. There had to be room for the personal, nerdy and occasionally slightly odd things that make the apartment ours: LEGO flowers and a LEGO Star Trek Enterprise, NASA posters, heavy glass candle holders I received as gifts, a strange glass sculpture from my former postdoc supervisor, my partner's small wooden seal from his late mother, and his amulets.

These objects are not exceptions to the design. They are part of it. The calmer surroundings are intended to give them room to exist without making the apartment feel cluttered.


## Current direction

Living in the apartment changed the project.

My original idea was probably closer to a showroom: the perfect Japandi catalogue interior, where every object had been carefully curated to conform to an aesthetic ideal that, in retrospect, I could not even have defined particularly well.

Then I moved in, and my things rather inconveniently moved in with me.

It turned out that many of them mattered far more to me than maintaining a perfectly consistent aesthetic. The glass sculpture from my former postdoc supervisor has genuine personal significance. My LEGO flowers were gifts from my partner, friends and family. The NASA posters fit the muted colour palette, but they also represent the astrophysicist in me. A wall now has three small pictures of Japanese grumpy toads hanging above a green plant, for no better design reason than that I love them.

The underlying aesthetic has not disappeared. Oak remains one of the dominant materials, including the shelving, sofa and dining chairs. The sofa is grey, the J48 dining chairs are anthracite, and the overall palette remains muted. Natural materials, simple forms and visual calm still provide the framework. But they are now a framework rather than a set of rules.

The same applies to new choices. I am considering New Yorker tiles for the kitchen, not because they are particularly Japandi, but because I like them and because they are practical and easy to clean. The aim is no longer to ask whether every individual choice belongs to a predefined style. It is to ask whether the choices work together, work for us and make the apartment a place I enjoy living in.

The apartment itself also has a very strong architectural feature that the interior should not compete with: the light and the view. The kitchen and living room have large west-facing panoramic windows overlooking a golf course and a park, with an uninterrupted view for several kilometres. Even after two years, my attention is still drawn towards the windows when I enter the room. I would generally rather watch the sunset than the television.

The long curtains are a muted dusty pink and plants add green throughout the apartment. Together with the oak, grey furniture and otherwise restrained palette, they add colour without overwhelming the rooms. The goal is increasingly not a perfect Japandi interior, but a calm and coherent home that leaves room for light, views, personality and the objects that tell our story.

## Principles and lessons learned

### Calm does not mean impersonal

The restrained palette and relatively minimal interior are not intended to remove personality. Their purpose is to create enough visual calm for meaningful objects to stand out.

### A style is a framework, not a rulebook

Japandi remains a useful reference for materials, colours and visual simplicity, but an object does not have to be "Japandi" to belong in the apartment. Personal significance, function and simply liking something are equally valid reasons.

### Work with the apartment

The light and the uninterrupted western view are among the apartment's strongest features. The interior should frame them rather than compete with them. Furniture placement, lighting and window treatments should preserve the openness of the space and make the changing daylight part of the interior.

### Function earns its place

A beautiful object that makes everyday life worse is not a successful choice. Practical considerations such as comfort, storage, cleaning and flexibility are legitimate parts of the design rather than compromises to it.

### A home is never finished

The aim is coherence, not a permanent final state. Objects, needs and people change, and the interior should be able to change with them.
